# The Stability of the U.S. Economy and the Role of National Debt

*Note: the figures in this analysis are drawn from 2020–2021 data.*

The stability of the U.S. economy matters to the entire world, simply because of its size: roughly 25% of the global economy.

Between the COVID-19 rescue packages and the economic policies of previous administrations, 2020 closed with a deficit of more than $3.1 trillion and a national debt of about $27 trillion. For scale, U.S. GDP in 2020 was approximately $20 trillion, which puts the annual deficit at about 15% of GDP. Growth in 2021 was projected at about 5%, lifting GDP above $21 trillion, with a deficit of roughly $2 trillion. Public debt was expected to rise by about 7%, pushing the national debt past $30 trillion, close to 150% of GDP.

What does that actually mean? Many will remember the informal 100% debt-to-GDP threshold Congress debated during the Obama administration. To answer the question properly, it helps to look at how that debt is structured.

About $6 trillion is intergovernmental debt, of which roughly $3 trillion sits in government trust funds, mainly reserves for Social Security and for civil and military retirement payments. A further $7 trillion is held by the Federal Reserve, and another $7 trillion by foreign entities. As of July 2020, Japan held approximately $1.29 trillion and China about $1.07 trillion, together roughly a third of all foreign holdings.

The remaining $7 trillion is held domestically:

* Mutual funds: $2.5 trillion
* State and local governments, including pension funds: $1.14 trillion
* Private pension funds: $819 billion
* Insurance companies: $244 billion
* U.S. savings bonds: $150 billion
* Other holders (individuals, banks, corporations and investment institutions): $1.79 trillion

Roughly 80% of the intergovernmental portion consists of long-term retirement and Social Security reserves. These funds are illiquid, so they have little short-term effect on the economy.

## Federal Reserve Holdings and Quantitative Easing

The Federal Reserve's holdings of U.S. debt come mostly from the quantitative easing policies introduced after the 2008 financial crisis and expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic. Total Federal Reserve assets grew from about $1 trillion in 2008 to more than $4 trillion by 2019, and passed $7 trillion during the pandemic, all visible in the Fed's balance-sheet data.

Those Treasury securities appear as assets on the Fed's balance sheet, but buying them created matching liabilities in the form of newly created money. Most of that money accumulated in the private sector and was deposited in commercial banks, many of which redeposited their excess liquidity back at the Federal Reserve as excess reserves. By mid-2020 those excess reserves had reached roughly $2.8 trillion, a direct reflection of how much liquidity the pandemic response had injected. The Federal Reserve began paying interest on them in order to keep monetary control and hold off excessive inflation.

## Asset Inflation and Wealth Redistribution

A large share of the new liquidity never reached productive investment. Corporations used the money to buy back their own shares or to invest in real estate, stocks, gold and cryptocurrencies, pushing asset prices up.

Quantitative easing therefore redistributed wealth. Anyone holding assets gained as prices rose; anyone holding cash or fixed-income savings lost ground, because interest rates stayed low.

## Savings Rates and Trade Imbalances

U.S. savings rates have historically run below those of major exporting countries such as China and Japan. During the pandemic they rose sharply for a time before falling back. The personal saving rate measures the share of disposable income that is not spent on consumption and is instead available for investment or financial markets.

The higher savings rate temporarily narrowed the U.S. trade deficit, but the long-term structural trend remains uncertain.

## Foreign Holdings and Global Financial Flows

Foreign entities hold roughly $7 trillion in U.S. debt, though a significant share of that is indirectly controlled by U.S. corporations through offshore subsidiaries in tax-efficient jurisdictions such as Ireland, Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands. Foreign investors also hold substantial amounts of U.S. equities and other financial assets, a measure of how attractive American markets remain.

## Trade Deficits and Currency Dynamics

Persistent U.S. trade deficits are structurally tied to the dollar's role as the global reserve currency. Export-oriented economies such as China and Japan hold their exchange rates at competitive levels, which sustains their trade surpluses and their steady accumulation of dollar reserves. Those reserves are then reinvested in U.S. financial assets, which reinforces dollar dominance in turn.

A significant move away from dollar reserves would likely depreciate the dollar. That would make U.S. exports more competitive, but it would also cut domestic purchasing power.

## Emerging Alternatives to Dollar Dominance

Some countries are looking for alternatives to dollar-based international transactions. China has reduced its Treasury holdings and is developing a digital yuan; the European Union is exploring digital currency initiatives of its own.

These may gradually erode dollar dominance, but they are unlikely to displace it quickly, given the dollar's institutional trust, liquidity and deep integration into global finance.

## Conclusion

On this analysis, U.S. national debt, large as it is, remains structurally sustainable, because global demand for dollar-denominated assets remains strong.

Persistent trade deficits and reliance on foreign capital do, however, create long-term structural problems:

* reduced domestic industrial capacity
* asset price inflation
* wealth inequality
* dependence on foreign capital inflows

In effect, the United States has served as the global consumer of everyone else's surplus production, sustaining high living standards at home while enabling export-driven growth abroad.

If confidence in the dollar erodes gradually, both the United States and the world economy can adapt. A sudden loss of confidence is another matter. That could destabilise the global economy severely.
