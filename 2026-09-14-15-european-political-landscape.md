# European Political Landscape

Since the dissolution of the Soviet Union, European policy has suffered from what might be called the "Fukuyama syndrome." Influenced by Francis Fukuyama, many believed that evil had effectively been eliminated, and that the "end of history" had arrived. On this view, ideological and religious fanaticism, imperial ambition (except the kind attributed to the evil United States), and great-power competition were all relics of the past. Nations would now cooperate harmoniously. Cultural differences would fade, and modern education systems and social services would resolve whatever tensions remained.

Europe did not fully wake up even after the Russian Federation annexed Crimea, or after the vicious terrorist attack on the French cartoonists at *Charlie Hebdo*. Instead, many policymakers went on trying to solve demographic decline by opening the borders to large-scale immigration from predominantly Muslim countries, often without clear quotas, educational requirements, or any consideration of cultural integration. Angela Merkel, though no socialist, adopted policies that in effect invited large numbers of Syrian refugees into Germany, and large numbers came. By the time the real wake-up call arrived with the Russian invasion of Ukraine, it was in many ways already too late.

Meanwhile, some Muslim clerics have begun assertively describing parts of Europe in terms of *Dar al-Islam*, a concept worth looking up. After the attacks by Hamas and many Gazan civilians on the Israeli villages near Gaza, and even before Israel had fully mobilised its response, large demonstrations by these Muslim communities took place across Europe, joined by the political left, chanting the slogan "From the river to the sea." In practical terms, that is a call for the elimination of the State of Israel and the annihilation of its ten million people, including roughly two million Arab citizens.

This, in essence, is the current European political landscape: from the "river" Rhine to the "sea" of the Atlantic Ocean.
