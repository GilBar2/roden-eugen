# Why Russia Achieves So Little With So Much

Recently I was asked: how can Russia project power globally when its economy is no bigger than Spain's? At first sight it seems paradoxical.

Russia is a country of 144 million people, only 79% of them ethnic Russians, ranked twelfth in the world by GDP. And yet it behaves like a great power, challenging NATO, shaping global politics, fighting wars and asserting imperial interests. The question is not how it does so much with so little. It is how it does so little with so much.

To understand the present, we have to see Russia not as an economy but as a historical identity: shaped by empire, by trauma, by territory, and by a permanent struggle to define itself between Europe and Asia.

## 1. The Shadow of Empire

Much of the post-Soviet space, including parts of Ukraine, Kazakhstan and other former USSR states, remains culturally and linguistically tied to Russia. Russian is still the second language across Eurasia, a legacy of imperial rule and Soviet hegemony.

Economically, though, Russia never became a modern consumer economy. The USSR was built around heavy industry, military production, and nuclear and space programmes. Consumer goods, market competition and the well-being of citizens were all secondary. The Soviet Union spent its national resources on ideology and geopolitics rather than on prosperity. The result was military might alongside household poverty.

## 2. After 1991: A Giant With Empty Shelves

When the USSR collapsed, Russia kept its global prestige, its land and its weapons. What it lost was Eastern Europe, Ukraine and Belarus (the traditional core), the Baltics, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and its ideological narrative. All of it went at once.

What emerged was a country selling raw materials, mainly gas and other natural resources, while importing consumer goods and technology: an economy the size of Spain's carrying the burdens of an empire. Outside Moscow and St Petersburg, most Russians live at standards among the lowest in the developed world.

The political structure survived too. Local party bosses became oligarchs, dissent stayed dangerous, and the state went on prioritising regime survival over prosperity. Putin's rule continues that tradition.

## 3. Talent Without Freedom

For all its structural problems, Russia remains extraordinarily creative. It produces outstanding scientists, writers, musicians and artists. American universities are full of Russian academics, many of them Jewish.

Israel is the clearest example. In the 1990s a million Russian Jews arrived and dramatically accelerated Israeli achievement in the economy, in science, in technology and in the arts. Russia had supplied the talent; Israel supplied the freedom to use it.

Great resources plus great talent plus weak institutions equals wasted potential. Russia exports brains instead of ideas.

## 4. A Cultural Empire With an Inferiority Complex

Russia has a glorious cultural history (Tolstoy, Tchaikovsky, Tarkovsky), and yet modern Russia has almost no cultural influence beyond its own borders. Its elites admire Western art, fashion, cinema and lifestyle. Even Russian intellectuals dream of Paris.

There is an old Soviet joke I once heard: "Today I would like to visit Paris again." They had never been there. They only longed for it.

## 5. Why Anti-Western Policy Persists

The anti-Western stance is not ideological. It is an instrument of internal control. If Russians saw the West clearly, the regime would collapse morally. In democratic nations politics reflects the people; in Russia it mostly reflects elites protecting their power, not citizens seeking freedom.

But the empire is ageing. The population is declining fast, Siberia is emptying out next to a booming China, the younger generation knows far more about the outside world, and war is becoming economically unsustainable as a tool. In the long run Russia faces a choice: integrate with Europe, or be absorbed economically by Asia. Geography pushes it towards the West; history and pride push it away. Demography may end up deciding for it.

## 6. Europe and Russia: Two Boxers Holding Each Other Up

Europe and Russia are like two exhausted fighters after fifteen rounds, leaning on each other and still swinging, while China and the United States watch and applaud. Culturally they belong to the same civilisation; politically they remain trapped in mutual mistrust.

Most ordinary Russians, given the choice, would probably join Europe. But the Russian elites have no interest in surrendering their privileges, just as the wealthy European states resist opening their doors to the poorer ex-Soviet nations.

## 7. The Real Future Conflict Is Not East vs West

The great divide of the twenty-first century may not be NATO against Russia, or LEFL societies (liberty, equality, fraternity and legality) against WOLEFL societies (weak on those same values). It may instead be Europe, Russia, Japan and the United States, ageing, educated and stable, set against Africa and the Muslim world, which are young, unstable and growing fast.

Demography drives history. Technology accelerates it. Culture shapes its wounds.

Russia and Europe sit on the same side of the demographic boat, and still they punch each other while the waves rise around them. Russia could have been a technological superpower. It could have led in culture, science, space and literature. It could have been Europe's partner instead of its antagonist. Instead it chose empire over prosperity, control over creativity, propaganda over intellect.

So, back to the original question: how can Russia do so much with so little?

Perhaps the real question is how it managed to do so little with so much.
